# Middle Worldbuilding

## Core idea

Worldbuilding has three useful scales:

| Scale | Main question |
| --- | --- |
| Macro | Why is the world this way? |
| **Middle** | **How does the world actually function because it is this way?** |
| Micro | What is this specific person/place/object like? |

DragonSword is particularly effective in the middle.

## “Write the sequel”

Treat a worldbuilding premise as something people have already lived with.

```text
premise
→ consequence
→ adaptation
→ normalization
→ culture
→ present-day story
```

Example:

**Weak:** monsters exist.

**Write the sequel:** roads are patrolled, merchants hire escorts, villages maintain guards, children know dangerous regions, mercenaries make a living solving problems the state cannot reach.

The interesting material is often not the premise. It is what happened afterward.

## Why DragonSword's art direction matters

Hound13 presents Orbis as a radiant, warm fantasy world. That supports the worldbuilding instead of merely decorating it.

### It feels worth saving

The visible world emphasizes:

- meadows;
- roads;
- settlements;
- merchants;
- cooking;
- local errands;
- companions;
- ordinary travel;
- ruins embedded among lived-in places.

The Dragon threat therefore hangs over **a functioning life**, not an already-dead wasteland.

That gives catastrophe contrast.

## 60 years is an excellent distance

The prior Dragon crisis is old enough to become:

- history;
- monuments;
- institutions;
- received wisdom;
- simplified stories;
- inherited political meaning.

But it is recent enough that living memory may still exist.

Three possible relationships to the same event:

**old:** “I remember it.”  
**middle generation:** “My parents remembered it.”  
**young:** “We learned about it.”

That is fertile territory for disagreement between memory and myth.

## Storybook geography

Orbis often behaves more like an illustrated adventure world than a geographic simulation.

A short trip can contain:

**field → ruin → settlement → cave → monster → treasure**

That compression works because the art direction embraces stylization.

The goal is less:

> prove this geography is realistic

and more:

> make every place feel like it belongs to the same imagined world.

## Mercenary viewpoint

Starting near the bottom of the social hierarchy is useful.

The player encounters the world outward:

**local jobs**  
→ other mercenaries  
→ merchants  
→ guards/knights  
→ religion  
→ political conflict  
→ ancient history

The setting gets larger as Lute's social reach gets larger.

## Middle-worldbuilding questions for Orbis

### Security

- Who protects roads?
- Where does royal jurisdiction weaken?
- How are monster bounties priced?
- Who can legally hire armed mercenaries?
- What happens when two companies claim the same contract?

### Religion

- What legal authority does the Organa Order possess?
- Who defines heresy?
- Does royal law constrain inquisitors?
- Who controls sacred/Dragon materials?

### Economy

- How important is Dragon Dust?
- Who mines/collects/moves/sells it?
- Is it taxed, licensed, rationed or smuggled?
- What does the trading network know before the government does?

### Magic

- Who teaches it?
- Which uses are regulated?
- Does the military recruit specialists?
- What professions exist only because magic is normal?

### History

- What do children learn about the Six Heroes?
- Who maintains their monuments?
- Why does “Six Heroes” persist if one is absent from the monument?
- What regional memories disagree with the official story?

### Monsters

- How have farming, architecture, travel and folklore adapted?
- Which creatures are pests versus existential threats?
- What ordinary precautions are so normal nobody explains them?

### Ruins

- Who owns artifacts?
- Archaeologist, Crown, Order, merchant or finder?
- Are ruins preserved, exploited, sealed or looted?
- Which abandoned defenses reveal older political geography?

## Key principle

> **Show the consequences of the worldbuilding, not only its causes.**

A war becomes convincing through roads, memorials, missing people, institutions and arguments.

A religion becomes convincing through jurisdiction, rituals, architecture and social expectations.

Magic becomes convincing through professions and infrastructure.

Monsters become convincing through ordinary precautions.

That is the “middle” layer that makes Orbis feel inhabited.
